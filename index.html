01

Quick reference

The handful of rules that make the whole chart click. Everything below comes straight from the source chart's own notes.

📐 The one formula behind every number

drawing units = (dose ÷ vial size) × BAC-water units

Example: 0.5 mg dose from a 5 mg vial reconstituted with 2 mL (200 units) of BAC water → (0.5 ÷ 5) × 200 = 20 units.

1 mL BAC water = 100 units Values rounded to nearest 0.5

🎯 Aim for 5–30 units per dose

The chart is built around landing your draw in the 5–30 unit window. Too far below and the draw is hard to measure accurately; too far above and you're injecting an unnecessarily large volume of fluid.

Remember: adding more BAC water only changes the concentration — more fluid injected, not more medication.

💉 Syringe guidance

Use a 0.3 mL insulin syringe when your draw is 30 units or less — it's the most accurate for small draws.

0.5 mL and 1 mL syringes don't have 0.5-unit markings, so for any draw ending in 0.5, the 0.3 mL syringe is recommended.

⚖️ Too much vs too little BAC

Too much BAC: over-dilutes — you must inject far more fluid to reach your dose, which can cause irritation and pain.

Too little BAC: over-concentrates — can cause burning/irritation on injection. Higher doses (e.g. 10–15 mg) may need reconstituting to ~50 units/dose to avoid over-concentration.

What the colors mean — the chart's own rating system

Green — idealReconstitute to this when possible, based on individual dose.
Yellow — common rangeConcentrations in the common range, but not necessarily ideal — depends on individual circumstances.
Orange — possible, not idealResulting units may be too low or too high to be fully accurate.
Red — avoidToo much or too little BAC for that dose. Do not reconstitute to these levels.

04

How the numbers are calculated

Every value in the chart comes from three simple steps. Worked here with the source chart's own example: a 5 mg vial, 2 mL of BAC water, 0.5 mg dose.

Find the concentration

Divide what's in the vial by how much BAC water you add. This tells you how much medication is in each mL.

5 mg ÷ 2 mL = 2.5 mg per mL

Find the fluid volume for your dose

Divide your dose by the concentration. This tells you how many mL of fluid carry your dose.

0.5 mg ÷ 2.5 mg/mL = 0.2 mL

Convert mL to syringe units

On an insulin syringe, 1 mL = 100 units. Multiply — then round to the nearest 0.5, the smallest marking you can measure.

0.2 mL × 100 = 20 units → draw to the 20 mark

05

Important notes

The source chart's own notes and fine print, plus what this reference does and doesn't cover.

📏 Rounding & syringes

All unit draws are rounded to the nearest 0.5, because 0.5 is the smallest unit measurement available on an insulin syringe. For any draw ending in 0.5, use a 0.3 mL insulin syringe (assuming the draw is 30 units or less) for the most accurate draw — 0.5 mL and 1 mL syringes don't have 0.5-unit markings.

🎯 The 5–30 unit target

Aim to reconstitute so each dose lands at 5–30 units. The chart's example: a 5 mg vial with a 0.5 mg dose — adding anywhere from 0.5–3 mL of BAC gives a draw in that usable range. Adding more BAC only changes the concentration — it means more fluid injected, not more medication. For higher doses (10–15 mg), reconstituting to ~50 units/dose may be needed to keep the dose from being over-concentrated.

⚠️ Balance the BAC volume

Too much BAC over-dilutes: you must inject far more fluid to reach your dose, which can cause irritation and pain, and is unnecessary. Too little BAC over-concentrates: it can cause burning/irritation on injection. Higher doses may require reconstitution to higher units.

🚦 The color system

Red = too much or too little BAC for that dose — do not use. Orange = possible but not ideal (draw may be too small or too large to measure accurately). Yellow = common range, not necessarily ideal. Green = ideal — use when possible for your dose.

📦 Coverage & limits

This reference covers vial sizes 2–60 mg and doses 0.05–15 mg — the full range of the source chart. Where a dose is larger than the vial, no value is shown. BAC water is assumed at 100 units per mL. This is a faithful interactive reproduction of a third-party chart: it doesn't replace the product's own labeling, a pharmacist, or a clinician.
